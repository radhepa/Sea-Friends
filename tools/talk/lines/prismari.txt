# Prismari: the shared pools every Prismari falls back to (jobs, the Oldest Eye and the young add their own).
#
# CANON. Keep every Prismari file consistent with this block and with the lectern books in
# tools/villages/designs/prismari.py ("The Long Tide", "The Reckoning of Tides", "The Halls We Left").
#
# From the books and signs:
# - The Long Tide: the age of the great halls (the monuments). It lasted ten thousand Long Counts. The Silence
#   lasted one. Since the leaving: four hundred and twelve (the lathe hall's Pact crate is marked "Count 412").
# - The Reckoning: one tide = a rising and a falling; two tides = a day; twenty-eight tides = a turning; thirteen
#   turnings = a Long Count (about half a land year). The pull of the water is felt in the spines. A young one is
#   "given their count" when they first feel the pull; when an Oldest "stops counting" (dies), the court counts for
#   them. "No tide goes unkept."
# - The halls: the Hall of the Green Lamp, the Hall of Spines, the Deep Gate, the Seat of the Eldest, the Cold Hall,
#   the Hall of Many Pillars, the Sponge Vaults, the Last Hall (where the Silence began), and others whose names
#   went into the dark with their keepers.
# - Guardians are the watch-beasts. Elder guardians are "the eldest", set at the hearts of the halls; in the dark
#   they "went strange". "The beasts remember the Seal, not our faces."
# - Sponges are breath-stones: a dry one drinks a sealed room dry so guests who breathe air can stand in it. The
#   halls' gold was tribute, "not ours to take back"; "Two-Eyes take the gold. Let them carry it, if they can."
# - The Pact of Lanterns with the merfolk: "our light for their friendship" (conduits, Tide Lamps).
# - The Archive's barred shelf: "THE SILENCE. Sealed by the Oldest Eye. Do not read."
#
# Invented here:
# - OSSIVETH: the last Oldest Eye of the halls. Sat in the Seat of the Eldest, led the leaving, sealed the first
#   record of the Silence. Looked back once, at the Deep Gate: the histories record the look, not what was seen.
#   Stopped counting in the first Long Count after the leaving. Every Oldest since seals the Silence's shelf again,
#   unread, on the day they become Oldest. Whether the Seat was named for the Oldest Eye who sat in it or for the
#   eldest beast that lay beside them, the Archive keeps both opinions.
# - The halls in the old tongue (use sparingly): Ithra Velune (the Hall of the Green Lamp), Ithra Kessuth (the Hall
#   of Spines), Omvarra (the Deep Gate), Saelorun (the Seat of the Eldest), Ithra Hesk (the Cold Hall), Ithra Tammun
#   (the Hall of Many Pillars, where the old Archive stood), the Lummaren (the Sponge Vaults). The Last Hall's own
#   name is never said aloud, except once, by each Oldest Eye to the next. ithra = hall. velunai = "lit by someone else's lamp" (said of a dear friend).
# - Nobody now living has been inside a hall or seen one lit up close. A Prismari lives some three hundred Long Counts.
# - The gold was tribute from the shore-kings: land rulers who came down to the halls for light in the Long Tide and
#   breathed in rooms drained for them. Their cities are sand now.
# - Greeting "I see you." Answer "And I you." Farewell "Stay seen." (do not be forgotten). The worst thing to be is
#   unseen: the dead are counted, the unseen are only missing.
# - A slow blink is a smile. Spines rise when hurt, angry or afraid (sometimes when glad); remarking on someone's
#   spines is rude, and everyone does it. The young have soft spines that set hard when they come of count.
# - They no longer sing (the halls were full of song before the Silence); they hum. The merfolk sing for both.
# - No family names ("Vaelion of the Court of Still Water"). No doors in a court: "welcome works better". The
#   tide-meal, twice a day (dried kelp, raw cod). Cooking with fire is a Two-Eyes oddity. The Eye Spire is the
#   court's open eye; its heart (the conduit) never goes dark; the court gathers there in the evening to hear the
#   Oldest tell the Long Tide, word for word. Every spire keeps a niche of breath-stones for Two-Eyes guests.
# - The drowned are "the Unseeing": two eyes each, and they see nothing. The Eyeguards' oath: "We watch so the court
#   may close its eye." The eldest beasts' curse is "the eldest's weariness" (arms heavy, tools slow).
# - The merfolk: "the reef cousins", the Tide-taken, young to the sea since the Great Flood. The Prismari met them new
#   to their tails and afraid of the dark. The merfolk carry the Prismari's wet breath-stones up to the sun to dry.
#   A crate of Tide Lamps goes up to the reef each turning, the count written on its side. The Pact was sworn at the
#   edge of the deep: the merfolk sang, the Prismari kept silent, and both counted as an oath.
# - Prismarine "thinks" (its colour never settles). Glowjellies never sting Prismari; nobody knows why.
# - The young practise the stillness: sit unmoving and unblinking by the jelly pond until a glowjelly drifts against
#   them. Their toys are little prismarine guardians.
# - Proverbs: "Still water sees farthest." "Count the tide, not the wave." (do not fret over small things) "A raised
#   spine is a word unsaid." "Light given is light kept." (the Pact) "What the deep keeps, the deep keeps." (do not
#   pry). The Archivists' addition to the second: "But record the wave, in case."
# - The Silence is only ever HINTED at: it began in the Last Hall; it lasted one Long Count; afterwards there were
#   fewer Prismari than halls; the tides of it are written in hands that grow shorter, then one careful new hand; the
#   Archive cannot agree whether it came in through the Deep Gate or began within; the bars on the sealed shelf are
#   not there to keep the books in. Never explain it.

## prismari.chat
We count by tides. One tide is a rising and a falling; two make your day. It is more honest than counting by a sun you cannot see.
Twenty-eight tides make a turning, and thirteen turnings make a Long Count. Ask me my count and I will tell you. Ask me my age and I will look at you strangely.
You keep blinking. Is something wrong with your eyes, or is it only that you have two of them to keep wet?
Still water sees farthest. That is the first thing our young are taught, and the last thing anyone learns.
Count the tide, not the wave. It means: do not fret over small things. I say it to myself often, and at length.
My spines are a terrible secret-keeper. Whatever I feel, they announce to the whole court.
Every lamp in {place} faces outward. That is not for us. That is so the dark knows we are here and are not afraid of it.
We keep no family names. I am {name} of {place}, and that is enough to be found by.
You land folk build up, toward the sun. We built down and out, toward the quiet. Both are reasonable.
A court is small. Everyone knows whose spines rose at the tide-meal, and why.
The tide-meal is taken twice a day, once for each tide. Dried kelp, a little cod. Your kind cook things with fire, I hear. That seems like an argument with the food.
We do not sing. We hum, sometimes, under our breath. The merfolk sing enough for both peoples.
The Eye Spire is the court's open eye. While its heart burns, {place} is awake, even when all of us sleep.
To blink slowly at someone is to smile at them. You have been smiling at everyone all day. It is very confusing.
Gold does not rust and does not rot. That is the whole of its virtue, and the reason it was given as tribute.
A raised spine is a word unsaid. Remember that when you meet an Eyeguard.
The sponges in our gardens are alive, you know. The slowest creatures in the sea, and the most generous.
If you ever lose your way in the deep, swim toward the light that does not flicker. That will be us.
I have been told your kind sleep for a third of every day. A third! What do you see in there?
We say "I see you" because it is the most anyone can promise: I have noticed you, and I will not pretend I have not.
When we part, we say "stay seen". It means: do not be forgotten. Do not go where no one will look for you.
The worst thing a Prismari can be is unseen. Not dead: unseen. The dead are counted. The unseen are only missing.
People ask why the deep is so cold. I tell them it is keeping things fresh. Nobody believes me, but they stop asking.
Your kind have two eyes, so you can look at two things and believe neither. We look at one thing until it tells us the truth.
Some rooms of the old halls were drained dry for guests like you, with breath-stones in the walls and air you could stand in. We were good hosts, once. We are trying to be again.
We do not go near the old halls. Please do not ask me why. My spines will answer for me, and they are not polite.
Patience is cheap in the deep. We have so much of it that we give it away.
The light in a court never goes out. That promise is older than {place}.
I like your kind. You are always in a hurry, and it is restful to watch someone else be in a hurry.
Every court has an Oldest Eye who keeps the Long Tide and tells it again in the evenings, in the same words, so that nothing slips.
The glowjellies drift where they please. We built the pond around wherever they happened to be. That is how most of {place} was planned.
Prismarine never settles on one colour. Watch a block of it long enough and it changes its mind. Our Lightwrights say it is thinking.
We do not have a word for "hurry". We have a word for "the current is strong today", which is not the same.
A court is quiet, but never silent. We are careful with that word.
Our histories are written very small, so that they take less space and more time to read. Both are virtues here.
Every eye in a court is its own colour: gold, sea-green, violet, rose, pale blue. They run in lines, like tides. Yours, I see, come in pairs.
I was told your kind cannot see in the dark. Then how do you know where the dark ends?
The guardians in the old halls were bred by us, long ago. Our watch-beasts. They still keep the halls, though there is nobody left to keep them for.
Do not be alarmed by how slowly we talk. We are thinking of the next tide, not the next breath.
In the deep, a thing said once is said for a long time. So we say few things, and choose them.
You are welcome to the court's light, its kelp and its quiet. The quiet is the best of the three.
If a Prismari offers you a breath-stone, take it. It means they would rather you lived.
Every spire keeps a niche of breath-stones for Two-Eyes who need to rest. It went unused for many turnings. I am glad it is useful again.
The young practise the stillness: they sit by the jelly pond until a glowjelly drifts against them. Some sit there for many tides. Some of us are still waiting.
We keep every tide. Even the dull ones. Especially the dull ones; they are so easy to lose.
Prismari do not shout. If you ever hear one of us shout, swim the other way. Quickly.
A stranger once asked what the eye on our banners means. It means we are watching. It also means we are watching.
In a court, nothing is wasted. Old lamp-cages become pond-rims. Old robes become kelp-cloth again. Old arguments become proverbs.
You are looking at my spines. It is rude to stare at spines. It is also rude to stare at eyes. We are a difficult people to look at.
We count the merfolk among our oldest friends, though they are a young people. To us, almost everyone is a young people.
What the deep keeps, the deep keeps. It means: do not pry. It is also, I admit, what we say when we have lost something.
Light given is light kept. That is the heart of the Pact of Lanterns, and the reason every court has a lamp to spare.
We are a long-lived people, so we try not to make friends carelessly. Then someone like you swims in, and all our care goes to waste.

In {place}, a stranger is a guest until they prove otherwise. Few prove otherwise. Fewer prove it twice.

Our ways are simple: greet with "I see you", answer with "and I you", and leave the glowjellies alone.

## prismari.past
In the Long Tide we raised the great halls of the deep. Your kind call them monuments. We called them home, and we were never cold in them.
The Long Tide lasted ten thousand Long Counts. The Silence lasted one. Since the leaving we have counted four hundred and twelve. We are still counting.
The guardians were our watch-beasts. We bred them for loyalty, and they gave it. They give it still, to halls with nobody in them.
The eldest beasts lay at the hearts of the halls. They were wise, in their way. In the dark they went strange. I do not think it was their fault.
Some say the eldest went strange because we left. Some say we left because they went strange. The Archive does not say which.
The Silence began in the Last Hall. That is all the histories say, and all I will say, and my spines would thank you not to ask again.
The halls were full of song once. We have not sung since the leaving. We hum. It is not the same, but it is ours.
Ossiveth was the last Oldest Eye of the halls. It was Ossiveth who led the leaving, and Ossiveth who sealed the first record of the Silence. Every Oldest since has kept that seal unbroken.
They say Ossiveth looked back only once, at the Deep Gate. The histories record that look. They do not record what Ossiveth saw.
The gold in the old halls was tribute, brought down by the shore-kings in the Long Tide, when the land still came to us for light. Their cities are sand now. The gold is still there.
In the Long Tide our sponges filled whole vaults; the Sponge Vaults, we called them. Guests came down from the land and breathed in rooms we had drained for them.
The halls had names. The Hall of the Green Lamp. The Hall of Spines. The Deep Gate. The Seat of the Eldest. The Cold Hall. The Hall of Many Pillars. We say them so they are not lost.
Some halls' names went into the dark with their keepers. When an Archivist finds one in an old ledger, the whole court gathers to hear it said aloud.
After the Silence there were fewer of us than there were halls. Count the courts, count the halls, and you will see why we do not speak of it.
We met the merfolk when they were new to their tails and afraid of the dark. We gave them light. They gave us the first warm voices we had heard since the leaving. That was the Pact of Lanterns.
The first courts were built in sight of nothing at all, on purpose. The old halls glow. We wanted a place where nothing glowed but us.
In the Silence, the count was kept by whoever was left to keep it. Some of those tides are written in hands we cannot name. We keep them anyway. No tide goes unkept.
Our histories are careful, not secret. There is a difference. A secret hides something. Care only waits.
The eldest beasts were never cruel. I think, in the dark, they simply forgot whom they were guarding against.
We have never agreed whether the Silence came in through the Deep Gate or began within. The Archive keeps both opinions, on different shelves.
The Seat of the Eldest was where Ossiveth sat. Whether it was named for the Oldest Eye or for the eldest beast that lay beside them, the Archive keeps both opinions, and its spines are up about it.
In the Long Tide, the Lightwrights of the Hall of the Green Lamp could turn a lamp in a single tide. We still have the lathes. We do not have the hands.
After the leaving, the first thing the courts made was light. Before beds, before walls. You cannot count tides in the dark.
The first lamp given under the Pact of Lanterns still burns in some reef garden, they say. Nobody knows which. Every reef claims it.
When we left the halls, we left the Sponge Vaults full and wet. Nobody went back to dry them. Perhaps they are drinking still.
In the Long Tide we needed no Eyeguards; the beasts watched the halls for us. When we left them, we had to learn to watch for ourselves. We are still learning.
The first Eyeguards stood at the Deep Gate during the leaving, to see everyone out. The Archive names every one of them. Every Eyeguard knows those names by heart.
The trident is older than the halls. We carried it when we first came down into the deep, before we had built anything at all.
The Halls We Left names eight halls and admits it does not know the rest. The Archivists say it is the most honest book they keep.
The tides of the Silence are written in many hands, one after another, each shorter than the last. Then a new hand takes over and writes very carefully. That is all anyone will say of them.
In the Long Tide, the Archive was the Hall of Many Pillars, and every pillar was a shelf. When we left, we carried what we could. We could not carry the pillars.
When we left the halls, we left the lamps lit, so the beasts would not be alone in the dark. The lamps burn still. The eldest went strange anyway. Light is not company.
In the old tongue the Deep Gate is Omvarra, and the Hall of Spines is Ithra Kessuth. The Last Hall has a name too. We do not say it.

## prismari.night
At night the court's lamps seem to lean a little closer together. It is a good time to be quiet.
We sleep with the eye shut, which takes more trust than you might think. That is why the Eyeguards watch.
Night does not change the deep, only the people in it. Listen: you can hear the court breathing.
If you cannot sleep, sit by the jelly pond. The jellies do not care whether you sleep. It is very restful, not being cared about.
In the old halls, they say, the eldest beasts never slept. I hope that is not true. I hope they rest.
A Prismari asleep looks exactly like a Prismari thinking. You will have to take my word that I am awake.
The second tide is falling. Far above, the moon is pulling at the sea, and I can feel it in my spines like a hand on my back.
You should rest too. Your kind breathe so much more than ours; it must be exhausting.
The watch-eye on the Eyeguards' post never closes. It is only a carving. At night, it is a comfort anyway.

## prismari.work
Every Prismari has work, and the work is the same: keep the court lit, keep the count, keep the guests breathing.
Work is slow in the deep. That is not idleness. A thing done slowly is done once.
I am at my task. You may watch. Watching is a respected art here.
Between the tides there is work, and between the work there is stillness. A good day has both, in the right order.
Each turning a crate of lamps goes up to the reef. Everyone in {place} has a hand in it, even if only to carry.
The court works so that the Eye Spire never goes dark. That is the whole of our labour, once you take away the details.
Forgive me if I do not stop. The tide does not stop for me either.
Our hands are webbed. It makes fine work harder and holding things under water much easier. We have decided it is a fair trade.
Ask the court what it needs and it will say "nothing". Then it will show you a list.
I work by the pull of the tide. When it slackens, I rest. When it turns, I begin again. Your kind have bells for this, I hear.

## prismari.adventure
If you go to the old halls, go with the Seal or do not go. The beasts remember it. They do not remember mercy.
The eldest beasts lay a weariness on anyone who comes near the hearts of the halls. Your arms grow heavy and your tools grow slow. Do not dig there. Only leave.
In the deep, carry a light that does not mind the water. A Tide Lamp, a sea lantern. Your little fires will only sulk.
The Unseeing walk the sea floor at night: the ones you call drowned. Two eyes each, and they see nothing. Keep a trident between you and them.
Two-Eyes take gold from the old halls. We do not stop them. It was tribute, and it is not ours to take back. Carry it, if you can.
A good traveller in the deep breathes before they need to. A wise one buys a breathing brew from the merfolk Tide Singers first.
Swim the long way round the old halls. The short way has eyes in it.
If the guardians see you without the Seal, they will not ask your business. They were bred not to ask.
The deep is wider than any land. Go, by all means. But tell someone in {place} which way, so that if you do not come back, you are counted, not lost.
A conduit's light keeps breath and sight in the deep. The merfolk keep one in every Reef Town. Swim from light to light and you will not go far wrong.
Watch the bubbles that rise from the floor. Some will lift you; some will drag you down. The deep is full of currents with opinions.
Do not stay in a hall past a tide. That is not a rule of ours. It is only what we have noticed about the ones who did.
The deep is not cruel. It is only uninterested in whether you live. Bring your own interest.
If you find writing in the old halls, any writing at all, bring it to an Archivist. Do not read it first.

## prismari.joke
How many Two-Eyes does it take to see a thing clearly? Two, apparently, and they will still disagree.
A young one asked why guardians have one eye and spines. "We made them to look like us," I said. "Then why do they bite?" I changed the subject.
The merfolk asked us to teach them the stillness. After half a tide, three had fallen asleep and one was humming. We called it a success.
Our proverb says "Still water sees farthest." The merfolk version is "Still water is boring." Both are true.
I tried winking once, to see what Two-Eyes find so amusing about it. I could not see anything for a while.
There is one joke about the Silence. Nobody tells it.
A Two-Eyes once gave me a hat. It is still on my spines and will be until it rots. Nobody had the heart to tell them.
Our word for "sarcasm" is the same as our word for "a spine rising slowly". Draw your own conclusions.
I asked a merfolk child how old they were. They said "nine". Nine what? Tides? Turnings? They swam off laughing. I am still waiting.
The best thing about one eye: nobody can ever call you cross-eyed. The worst: you cannot wink. We have tried. It is a long, uncomfortable blink.
A guardian, an eldest and a Two-Eyes swim into a hall. Only one swims out, and it is never the one carrying the gold. That is not a joke. It is a warning with a rhythm.
You have two eyes. Do you ever close one to rest it and keep watch with the other? No? What a waste.
How many Lightwrights does it take to light a court? One, and a hundred tides of everyone else saying it is too bright.
The Spongekeepers want less water. The Lightwrights want more light. Together they would make the land, and nobody wants that.
A Spongekeeper's favourite joke is very long and has no ending. It soaks up an entire evening.

## prismari.heart

I would like, once, to hear the song the halls were filled with. Nobody remembers it. Somewhere in the dark, perhaps, the eldest beasts do.

## prismari.neighbors
The merfolk are our friends under the Pact of Lanterns: our light for their friendship. We have both kept our side, which is more than most pacts can say.
We gave the merfolk their conduits. They gave us noise, gossip, songs and kelp wraps. I think we got the better bargain.
The merfolk are a young people. The Tide took them in the Great Flood and gave them tails. To us they are still new to the water. To them, we are very old furniture.
A merfolk greets you with "fair currents" and leaves with "mind the undertow". We greet with "I see you" and leave with "stay seen". Between us, we have the whole sea covered.
Their Tide Singers keep the conduits we gave them, and sing to them. We never sang to ours. Theirs seem happier.
The merfolk carry our wet breath-stones up into the sun to dry. We cannot go up there. They do it for a song, which they sing on the way.
When the merfolk come down to trade, the court is noisier for a tide. We complain about it for a turning. We miss it for the rest of the Long Count.
Their young are called fry. They ask questions without waiting for answers. Ours wait so long for answers they forget the question. We should swap for a turning.
The Reef Wardens and our Eyeguards drill together sometimes. The Wardens move like a struck lamp; the Eyeguards move like the tide. Both are very hard to get past.
The merfolk ride the wild nautili. We have tried. The nautili did not care for our spines.
Each turning, a crate of Tide Lamps goes up to the reef with the count written on its side. The merfolk never ask what the number means. They just say thank you. We like that about them.
The merfolk still keep land ways: names, gossip, feasts, family quarrels. We find it exhausting and wonderful, like weather.
A merfolk pearl diver once gave me a Tidepearl, for no reason at all. I keep it beside my bed. I still do not know what the reason was.
If you are going up to the reef, tell them {place} sees them. They will know what it means. They will say it back wrong, warmly.
The merfolk think the deep is frightening. We think the shallows are. We are both right, and we visit each other carefully.

A merfolk Elder came down to a court once and talked for three tides without stopping. The court still speaks of it with awe.
