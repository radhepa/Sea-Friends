# MERFOLK: shared pools every merfolk falls back to (diver, farmer, shellwright, singer, warden, elder, child).
#
# MERFOLK CANON (invented here; keep it the same across every merfolk file)
# - The Tide: the sea's own will. When the Great Flood drowned the old coast villages, the Tide "took" some of
#   the drowning and gave them tails; the ones it would not take came back as the drowned (pitied and feared).
#   Oaths: "Tide's teeth!", "Tide willing", "Tide keep you". Saying: "The Tide gives and the Tide keeps."
# - Nobody alive remembers the Flood; the Song does. The old coast villages: Saltcombe (everyone claims their
#   family came from Saltcombe), Gullstone, Brinewick, Hollin Bay. Every Reef Town keeps one bell from its old
#   land village, wrapped in kelp-cloth and brought out only on Bell Night.
# - Festivals: Bell Night (the Flood remembrance: the old bell is rung once for every lost name, until morning);
#   Lantern Night (the Pact festival: every family sets a sea pickle on the shrine steps, a Prismari envoy comes
#   up to "look upon the light", everyone sings "The Lantern Promise"); Blushing Day (a fry's pale silver tail
#   takes its family colour; a singer sings the family song and the fry gets a carved shell comb); the Long Cut
#   (the great kelp harvest: everyone hauls and sings "Haul the Green"); the Spiral Race (a nautilus race round
#   the reef).
# - Songs: "The Song of the Flood" (forty verses and a forty-first nobody sings; the Elders know it, won't say);
#   "Hush Now, Little Fin" (the lullaby, first song and last); "Haul the Green" (kelp work song); "The Lantern
#   Promise" (the Pact song, learned from the Prismari); "The Nautilus Who Wouldn't" (comic; the fry demand it).
# - Wenna Saltcombe: the first Tide Singer. She swam down into the dark with one lantern and sang until the
#   Prismari came up to see what was singing. The Pact of Lanterns: the Prismari gave each town a conduit
#   (merfolk call it "the Heart"); the merfolk keep the shallows lit, carry news of the sun down to the deep, and
#   never enter the old monuments. Her fry's tail went gold on the first Blushing Day.
# - Lost Lowmere: a Reef Town past the shelf whose singers forgot the songs (so the Elders say); its Heart went
#   dark (within the oldest Elders' memory) and the drowned took it. The Weeping Pearl (the greatest Tidepearl,
#   black as the deep and wet even out of water) went down with it.
# - The Barnacle King: a great old drowned with a crown of barnacles who sits on Lowmere's shrine. The fry's
#   bogey ("eat your kelp or the Barnacle King gets your fins"). Wardens say "fry story" and sharpen tridents.
# - Old Coil: the white nautilus as big as a cottage who carried the first fry down in the Flood, a dozen to a
#   shell. Never ridden since; seen out past the shelf on still nights; seeing her means a year's luck.
# - Old Pell (Pell Whelk): the wandering storyteller who swims reef to reef telling tales for supper; a famous,
#   beloved liar; does all the voices. "True as one of Pell's" means not true at all.
# - Sandtoes: the merfolk's fond word for land folk ("dry-walkers" is the old polite word). Merfolk are
#   fascinated by toes. The surface/land = "the dry". Goosebumps = "goose-scales". "Fins up" = cheer up.
# - Superstitions: never whistle over the drop-off (the shelf, where the deep begins: the deep thinks it's being
#   called); knock on coral three times before a long swim; never say "drown" at supper; sleep with your tail
#   toward the Heart (bad dreams go the other way); if the Heart hums off-key, someone nearby told a lie; a
#   seadragon in your garden keeps the drowned from your door; never chase a light in the deep; if a bell rings
#   at night that isn't yours, don't swim toward it; the Undertow is sly and listens ("Mind the undertow").
# - Bottle post: merfolk write to land cousins in bottles; old Letter Rocks at the tideline where the two
#   peoples once traded pearls for apples. When the first merfolk came ashore, the land folk brought blankets:
#   "remember the blankets".
# - Daily life: beds are sponge cushions; lamps are sea pickles (never eat a lit one); doors are kelp curtains;
#   kelp is dried on the sun-rocks at low tide; evenings at the shrine. Dolphins are the sea's worst gossips.
# - Tidepearls: a speck of prismarine crystal tucked into a basin clam and sung to for a moon; grades moonwhite,
#   dawnblush, deepblue (rarest: the clam had a sad moon). Every diver keeps one pearl they'll never sell.
# - Shellwrights: the Spiral Rule (never carve against a shell's spiral); Corran the Carver made the first
#   nautilus saddle from a cart seat out of the old village (every town claims to have it). Living coral is
#   never cut from the shrine. Singers: conch calls (one long blow: come to the shrine; two: danger; three:
#   supper); brews are "breath-brews" (for sandtoes) and "night-eyes" (for the watch).
# - Wardens: the Warden's Oath is "Hold the arch." The first wardens fought the drowned with fence posts.
# - Prismari: "I see you" is answered "And I am seen." The fry call them "Big Eyes" (never to their faces).
# - Hard times: "a pickle moon" (the year of the great storm, when the kelp tore loose and everyone ate pickles).

## merfolk.chat
Do you know what I'd give to see a sunset from the dry side? Well, not my tail. But nearly my tail.
My cousin lives in a land village. We send bottles. The last one said "it rained". I've read it a hundred times and I still don't really understand it.
Everyone thinks their tail is the best colour. Everyone is wrong, except me.
We knock three times on coral before a long swim. Is it silly? Yes. Have I ever skipped it? Never.
Kelp wraps for breakfast, kelp wraps for lunch, and on festival days, a kelp wrap with a little extra kelp.
The Heart hums all day. You stop hearing it, like your own heartbeat. Then it changes note and every head in town turns.
You land folk walk about on the bottom of the air. I think about that a lot.
Never whistle over the drop-off. The deep's listening, and the deep thinks it's being called.
Seadragons look like someone made a fish out of kelp and forgot to finish it. That's why we love them.
The nautili are terribly stubborn. You don't ride a nautilus so much as negotiate with one.
Old Pell the storyteller came through last moon and swore a whale once proposed marriage. Pell gets more wonderful every visit, and less true.
Do you know the trick to a good gossip down here? Lean in close. Sound travels under water, and so do secrets.
We sleep on sponge cushions. You float a little in the night and wake up somewhere slightly else. It's lovely.
The light comes down in ripples on a calm day. I could watch it for hours. I have, actually. Work did not get done.
My gran used to say the Tide gives and the Tide keeps. Usually right before taking the last kelp wrap.
Toes! What are they for? Truly. Do they help? Do you name them?
I tried walking once, on the beach at low tide. I flopped. I flopped with great dignity, mind.
There's no weather down here. There's just the surface going grumpy, and us looking up at it.
When the surface storms, the light goes dark green and the whole reef goes quiet. We sing louder then.
Sea pickles: lamp, snack and decoration, all in one. Don't eat the lit ones. You'll glow, and the fry will never let you forget it.
The shrine's where everyone ends up in the evening. Singing, gossiping, and pretending we came for the singing.
My tail's been itchy all week. The singers say it means a change in the current. My gran said it meant someone was talking about me. I'm going with the gossip.
Fry learn to swim before they learn to talk. Then they learn to talk and never stop.
Our town keeps a bell from the old land village, wrapped in kelp-cloth. It rings once a year. You'd never think a bell could sound sad until you hear it under water.
Some days I wonder what our old land village looked like. Then I look at the reef and stop wondering.
If the Heart ever hums off-key, somebody nearby just told a lie. So mind what you say near the shrine.
Leafy seadragons pretend to be kelp. You'd be surprised how much of the kelp in a garden is pretending.
The fathers carry the seadragon eggs, did you know? On their tails. Proud little things, swimming about with a tail full of babies.
People ask if it's cold down here. It's not cold. It's fresh.
The trick to living under the sea is not to think too hard about the surface. Also kelp. A great deal of kelp.
I polish my scales every evening. Vanity? Yes. But it keeps the barnacles off, and barnacles are worse than vanity.
You've got such a funny swim. All arms and kicking. It's very brave, considering.
I once dared myself to touch the top of the water. Just a fingertip. It was so dry up there! Like touching nothing at all.
My cousin up on land married a farmer. There's a bottle on my shelf that just says "harvest went well". I keep it like treasure.
There's an old saying: a seadragon in your garden, and no drowned at your door. Our garden's full of them. I sleep like a stone.
The deep past the shelf goes from blue to black, like a door closing. We don't swim through that door.
Gossip travels faster than the current here. By supper, everyone will know you came down. By morning, they'll know what you had for breakfast.
If you ever see a white nautilus as big as a cottage, that's Old Coil. Make a wish. Then swim away, very politely.
Do land folk have festivals? We've got Lantern Night and Bell Night and Blushing Day and the Long Cut, and every one has a song you can't get out of your head.
We don't have doors. Doors are for people with secrets. We have kelp curtains, which are for people with small secrets.
Nobody alive remembers the Flood. The Song does, though. That's what songs are for.
My scales go a little pale when I'm nervous. Don't tell anyone. They'll tease.
Have you tried a kelp wrap? Fish, kelp, a pinch of salt. Though down here, everything comes with a pinch of salt.
Tide keep me, I'd love to see snow. Is it cold rain? Bits of cloud? Nobody's bottle has ever explained it properly.
The fry dared each other to touch a pufferfish this morning. We now have three puffy fry and one very smug pufferfish.
You can tell how a merfolk's day went by the way they swim. Fast and straight, they're cross. Slow and loopy, it was a good one.
It's rude to swim over someone's dome without saying "fair currents". Nobody tells sandtoes that, so I'm telling you.
Every Reef Town claims to keep the oldest bell from the old coast. {place} actually does. Don't check.
I count the sea pickles on my path home every night. Same number every night. One night it won't be, and I'll know something's moved.
When I was small I thought the surface was a great silver ceiling and the sun was a lamp someone left on. I'm still not entirely convinced otherwise.
The singers say the Tide chose who got tails by listening to how we sang as we sank. I like to think my family sang something cheerful.
A good tail swish can say more than a sentence. That one means "I'm very interested". This one means "I'm being polite".
Nobody says "drown" at supper. It's bad luck, and it puts everyone off their wraps.
The drowned walk slow and they walk heavy. We swim. That's the whole difference, really, and it's everything.
Cousins on land send us apples in bottles sometimes. They arrive pickled. We eat them anyway, out of love.
You can always tell a nautilus that's been ridden by a warden. It swims like it's late for something.
Our beds are sponge cushions, our lamps are pickles and our doors are kelp. It's a soft sort of life. We like it soft.
The fry name every seadragon in the garden. One of them is called Lord Wiggles the Third. Nobody knows what happened to the first two.
Dolphins are the worst gossips in the sea. Worse than me! They'll tell the whole reef you came down before you've finished saying hello.
Lovely {time} for a swim, isn't it? Then again, they all are.
The best thing about a tail is the swish. The second best thing is the colour. The third best thing is never needing shoes.
I've heard land folk sleep lying flat, on top of their beds, and they don't drift at all. How do they know they've slept?
"Fins up," we say, when someone's low. It means cheer up. It also means "you're slouching", depending on who's saying it.
The current changes every evening, right when the songs start. My gran said the sea leans in to listen. I've never heard a better reason.
Up in the dry, you have to carry everything. Down here, if you let go of something, it just drifts about nearby, thinking it over.

## merfolk.past
The Song says the water came in one night and didn't go out in the morning. The old coast villages went under, bells and all.
When the Great Flood came, the Tide took some of the drowning and gave them tails. The rest... well. The rest are the drowned. We never laugh at them.
My family came from Saltcombe, on the old coast. Everyone's family came from Saltcombe, if you ask them. It must have been an enormous village.
Wenna Saltcombe was the first Tide Singer. She swam down into the dark with one lantern and sang until something sang back. That was the Prismari, and that was the Pact of Lanterns.
The first merfolk didn't know how to swim with tails. They wriggled about like eels for a moon, crying for their legs. Then they got the hang of it, and hardly anyone's cried for legs since.
Old Coil the white nautilus carried the first fry down from the Flood, a dozen to a shell, or so the Song says. She's never been ridden since.
There used to be a town called Lowmere, out past the shelf. Its singers forgot the songs, the Heart went dark, and the drowned came in. Now the Barnacle King sits on its shrine.
Every town keeps one bell from the old land villages. On Bell Night we ring it once for every name we lost. It takes until morning.
Our people kept the village ways because they were all we had left of the land. Names, gossip, festivals. Especially gossip.
They say the first Reef Town was built under the drowned roof of Saltcombe's market hall. The first Heart sat where the cheese stall used to be.
The Pact of Lanterns is simple: the Prismari gave us the Hearts, and we keep the shallows lit and carry them news of the sun. And we never go into the old monuments. Never ever.
My gran's gran's gran had legs. I think about that sometimes, looking at my tail. Then I think: the poor thing had to wear boots.
When the first merfolk went ashore to find their families, half the land folk screamed and the other half brought blankets. We remember the blankets.
Corran the Carver made the first nautilus saddle out of a cart seat from the old village. It's still kept in a shrine somewhere. Every town claims it's theirs.
In the old days there were no Tide Lamps and no Hearts, only sea pickles and singing. The deep was so much closer then.
The Song of the Flood has forty verses. There's a forty-first that nobody sings. The Elders know it. They won't say.
Before the Flood, Saltcombe held a fish market every seventh day. The fish have never forgiven us.
The first Blushing Day was when Wenna's own fry's tail went gold. She cried so hard the singers had to wait to start the song.
Old Pell says Lowmere still has its bell, and on Bell Night you can hear it ring from the wrong direction. Pell says a lot of things. That one, I believe.
The Tide didn't ask anyone if they wanted a tail. Some of the first merfolk were cross about it for years. Then they had grandchildren with tails, and that was that.
There was a time merfolk and land folk traded at the tideline every full moon: pearls for apples, shells for bread. Some of the old Letter Rocks are still there.
The first wardens fought the drowned with fence posts from the old villages. Tridents came later. Fence posts are surprisingly good, apparently.
The year of the great storm, the kelp tore loose all at once and the whole town lived on sea pickles for a moon. We still call a hard time "a pickle moon".
Old Pell remembers when there were half as many Reef Towns. Pell also remembers being friends with a whale, so, mixed.

## merfolk.night
At night the reef glows: pickles, lamps, the Heart, the jellies far down. You'd think the stars fell in.
Stay close to the light. The drowned don't like the Heart's glow, and I don't like the drowned.
Night's when the deep creeps a little closer to the shelf. It always goes back by morning. Always has.
Can't sleep? Me neither. I've counted every scale on my tail twice. Seven hundred and twelve. Then I lost count at the fluke.
If you hear a bell in the night that isn't ours, don't swim toward it. That's the old wisdom, and I'm sticking to it.
The fry sleep with their tails toward the Heart. Old habit. It keeps the bad dreams pointed the other way.
At night the surface goes black and silver and the moon comes down in pieces. It's my favourite thing in the sea.
The night watch says Old Coil went by the shelf last night, white as bone. Or it was a jellyfish. They'd been up a long time.
"Hush now, little fin, the Tide's rocking the reef..." Sorry. Lullaby. You hum it once and it's in you for life.
Nights are for sleeping, singing, and gossiping in whispers. Mostly the last one.
The glow squid come up the shelf on dark nights and hang there like lanterns nobody lit. Pretty, as long as you don't think too hard.

## merfolk.work
Everyone in {place} works: the divers dive, the farmers farm, the singers sing, and the fry get underfoot. It comes out about even.
Work's slow down here. You can't hurry water. The water won't let you.
We trade pearls and shells for what the dry sends down, and honestly, the dry gets the worse of it. Have you seen a good pearl?
If you want to help, haul kelp at the Long Cut. Everyone hauls. Even {elder} hauls, with commentary.
A good day's work under the sea ends with a song at the shrine. A bad day's work ends with a longer song.
We work from shimmer to dusk. The light tells us when to stop: when it goes from green to blue, tools down.
Nobody in {place} works alone. Even when you're on your own, the Heart's humming at you, so you're not really.
Land folk ask what we do all day. We work, we gossip, we sing. Same as you, but wetter.
You can't light a forge down here, so everything's carved, woven, grown or sung. It takes longer. It lasts longer, too.
If you see a merfolk resting, they're not lazy. They're listening to the current for work. That's what we say, anyway.
Our best trades go to friends. That's not a rule; that's just how hearts work.
Every job in {place} feeds the Heart somehow. Pearls for the Pact gifts, kelp for the brews, shells for the wardens. Even the gossip helps; it keeps everyone singing.

## merfolk.adventure
If you're after adventure, swim the reef edge at dusk. Everything's out: turtles, squid, and the odd drowned looking for a fight.
Past the shelf, the water goes black and cold. The divers go down on a rope of knotted kelp. I've never had the nerve.
The old coast villages are still down there somewhere, under the sand. Find one and bring me back a spoon. I've always wanted a land spoon.
Ride a nautilus full speed through the arches some time. You'll scream. Everyone screams. It's tradition.
If you go looking for lost Lowmere: don't. If you find it, swim away. If the Barnacle King sees you, swim faster.
There's a cave in the reef wall where the light comes in blue through little holes. The fry swear it sings. I've heard it. It's the current. Probably.
The ocean monuments? The Prismari's old halls, full of guardians. Don't. The guardians don't know you, and they don't care to learn.
If you swim far enough out, you'll find the shipwrecks. Land folk lost them; we leave them be. Respect, you see.
Want a real adventure? Talk a nautilus out of a sulk. Took me half a day once. Two pufferfish and a whole song.
Some say Old Coil sleeps in a cave beyond the kelp forests, in a nest of white shells. Find her and you'll never want for luck again. Or so we tell the fry.
The sea is bigger than the dry, you know. There's always one more thing past the next drop.
Never chase a light in the deep. Sometimes it's a jelly. Sometimes it's a Prismari. Sometimes it's neither, and that's the worst one.
You could try the long swim to the next Reef Town. Bring news, bring gossip, bring a pearl as a guest-gift. You'll be fed for a week.
Up for a dare? Touch the shelf edge at night and swim back without looking behind you. I did it once. I looked behind me.
Adventure's fine, but come back with all your fingers and both your funny feet. We've grown used to them.
The drowned like the old wrecks and the dark trenches. If you go poking about either, take a friend and a trident, in that order.

## merfolk.neighbors
The Prismari are our old friends in the deep. Tall, solemn, one great eye. When one says "I see you", you say "And I am seen". It matters to them.
A Prismari envoy comes up every Lantern Night to look upon our light. They don't smile. They don't need to. You can feel them being glad.
Never startle a Prismari. Their spines go up, and then you're apologising to a very tall, very spiky person for an hour.
The Prismari gave us the Hearts under the Pact of Lanterns. We keep the shallows lit and tell them about the sun. They love hearing about the sun.
The Prismari will call you "Two-Eyes". Don't take it badly. It's just accurate.
The Prismari count time in tides. Ask one how old they are and you'd better have a free afternoon.
They built the ocean monuments, long ago. Then something happened they call the Silence, and they left. They don't talk about it. Don't ask. Really, don't.
I asked a Prismari once what the surface was like. It said "bright, and brief". Then it looked at me with that eye until I had to go home and lie down.
If a Prismari Oldest Eye ever gives you their seal, the guardians at the monuments will let you be. Nobody I know has one. Nobody.
Their lamps are jellyfish. Living glowjellies, drifting in ponds. Don't touch. They sting anyone who isn't Prismari, and they sting with enthusiasm.
The Prismari dress in kelp-cloth trimmed with gold. Gold! Down there! They're the only folk I know who look formal while floating.
They speak so slowly and so properly. I once heard one say "the meal is acceptable". It was the most excited I've ever seen one.
The Prismari keep sponges they call breath-stones. In the old days they'd drain a whole room dry for a land guest. Imagine, a dry room at the bottom of the sea!
Our singers learned "The Lantern Promise" from the Prismari, note for note. When they sing it with us, the water shakes.
The fry call them "Big Eyes". Not to their faces. Never to their faces.

The Prismari won't go near the old monuments anymore. If the folk who built them won't go in, I'm certainly not.
Prismari Lightwrights make Tide Lamps that burn under water forever. We trade them pearls. It's the best bargain in the sea, for both of us.
