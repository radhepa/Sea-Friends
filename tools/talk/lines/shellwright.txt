## shellwright.chat
The Spiral Rule: never carve against a shell's spiral. The shell knows which way it grew, and it'll crack just to prove it.
Corran the Carver made the first nautilus saddle from a cart seat out of the old village. Every shellwright since has been trying to make one half as comfortable.
Living coral is never cut from the shrine. Never. We take from the edges, from storms, from what breaks on its own. The reef gives; we don't grab.
A nautilus saddle has to fit the shell exactly. Nautili are vain. If it pinches, they sulk for a week.
Shell armour's lighter than iron and it never rusts. It'll shatter if you're hit just right, though. So don't get hit just right.
I carve the shell comb for every Blushing Day in {place}, each one in the fry's own tail colour. Gold's the hardest to match. Gold families are fussy.
Coral goes pale if you take it out of the water. Then it's dead, and it's chalk. Keep coral wet and it keeps its colour forever.
The best spear points are shell, ground thin. They don't rust, they don't dull, and they look gorgeous on a wall.
Every shell's got a story. This one was a nautilus's spare room. This one was a clam's front door. This one was somebody's lunch.
The bone blocks we use for frames come from old whale bones on the sea floor. Whales are generous, even after.
Land folk use hammers. We use patience and a sharp bit of shell. It took me a week to make that cup. It's a very good cup.
I make nautilus armour from overlapping plates of shell, like the scales of a tail. The nautilus thinks it's being complimented.
The wardens keep breaking their spears on drowned and bringing me the pieces like it's my fault.
Fire coral's the hardest to carve and the prettiest to look at. Like some people I know.
I'm making a shell flute. It plays one note. It's a lovely note. I'm hoping for a second one eventually.

## shellwright.work
Carving shell is slow work. You don't cut it so much as persuade it.
Today I'm fitting a saddle. The nautilus is fitting a sulk.
I trade coral, shells, nautilus saddles and armour, and spears. Also advice on spirals, free, whether you want it or not.
When I'm not carving, I'm polishing coral. Sand, then grit, then a soft scrap of sponge. Then admiring. Admiring takes longest.
You measure a nautilus for armour plate by plate, round the spiral. It takes three people and a great many pufferfish.
I need coral stock for the Lantern Night lamps. Every family wants theirs carved special. I have never once finished on time.
A spear for a warden takes four days. A spear for a fry takes one; it's a lot shorter and a lot more blunt.
Brain coral for the bowls, fan coral for the screens, tube coral for the pipes. Every coral has a job if you listen to it.
This bench was my teacher's, and their teacher's before them. Every nick in it is somebody's mistake. Some of them are mine.
Someone's asked me to make a saddle for Old Coil, in case she's ever found. I've started. It's enormous. My workshop is full of a saddle for a legend.
Shell dust gets in everything. My wraps, my hair, my cushion. I sparkle in my sleep.
Bring me nautilus shells and I'll make you something worth bringing them for. That's the shellwright's promise.
Armour first, then spears, then pretty things. That's the proper order. I break it every day. Pretty things are more fun.
I'm restringing a warden's shield. They let a drowned chew on it. Chew! On my work!
Finishing is the best part: the last polish, holding it up to the Heart's light, seeing it catch. Worth all the dust.

## shellwright.heart
I keep the first thing I ever carved. A lopsided little fish. My teacher said it looked like a sad bean. I still love it.
When someone wears something I made, I feel like I'm out on the reef with them. It's nice, not being stuck at the bench.
The reef gives us everything we carve. I try to give something back: a shell on the garden bed, a bit of coral replanted. It's only fair.
I'd carve you anything, {player}. Anything. Just ask. Well, not a house. Not this moon.
Some days the shell cracks wrong and I want to throw the lot into the deep. Then I remember the Spiral Rule and start again, slower.
